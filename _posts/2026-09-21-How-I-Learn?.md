Over the years, I have developed a way of learning that works naturally for me. I did not create this process as a formal method; I gradually discovered it through the way I have learned different subjects. Looking back, I can now recognize the pattern clearly and use it intentionally whenever I begin learning something new.

My learning usually starts with **time and exposure**.

When I begin a difficult subject, I spend a significant amount of time with it. I read, think about it, revisit the same concepts, and allow myself to sit with the subject for long periods. In the beginning, the material is unfamiliar, so understanding does not come immediately. The purpose of this stage is not to master the subject at once, but to become familiar enough with it for my mind to begin building a structure around it.

## Building Mental Space

With continued exposure, the subject gradually becomes more familiar. Terms that were initially unfamiliar become recognizable, and individual concepts begin to connect.

I start building **mental space** for the subject. New information has somewhere to fit because I have already developed some understanding of the basic structure.

As this structure develops, learning becomes easier. I can relate new concepts to things I already understand instead of treating every piece of information as something completely new.

This is the stage where information starts turning into a **mental model**.

I begin to understand not only what something is, but why it exists, what problem it solves, how it works, and how it relates to the rest of the subject.

## Reaching the Blockers

Once I have built a reasonable understanding, I naturally reach more difficult areas. I encounter concepts that do not immediately fit into my existing mental model or problems that require more reasoning than simply reading the material.

These blockers are an important part of my learning process.

They force me to think more deeply. Instead of simply taking in information, I have to investigate the gap in my understanding. I ask questions, examine assumptions, connect different concepts, and try to work out why something behaves the way it does.

This is usually where the subject becomes cognitively engaging for me.

## Developing Interest

My interest often develops after I have already spent considerable time learning.

Once I understand enough of the subject to recognize its structure, I can also see the parts that I do not yet understand. Those gaps create questions, and the questions create curiosity.

This means I do not need to wait until I feel interested before starting something new.

I can begin with discipline and exposure. As my understanding develops, genuine interest can emerge naturally from the questions I encounter.

**For me, interest is often a consequence of understanding rather than a prerequisite for beginning.**

## Moving Toward Mastery

Once curiosity develops, I naturally want to go deeper. Reading alone is no longer enough, so I begin applying what I have learned.

I solve problems, experiment, implement concepts, make mistakes, investigate why something did not work, and return to the underlying theory when necessary. This cycle strengthens the mental model and turns theoretical knowledge into practical ability.

Eventually, I reach a point where I can reconstruct what I have learned without depending entirely on the original material. I can explain the concept, apply it to unfamiliar problems, connect it with other areas, and continue learning independently.

That is where I consider mastery to begin.

## The Process I Want to Carry Forward

Looking back, my learning process can be summarized as:

**Exposure → Mental Space → Familiarity → Mental Model → Blockers → Curiosity → Practice → Mastery**

This is not a process I need to reinvent every time I start something new. It is the process I have already used to learn over the years.

**I know my process now, so I can carry it deliberately into whatever I choose to learn next.**
