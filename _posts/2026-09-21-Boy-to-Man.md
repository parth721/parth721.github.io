# What Life Taught This Boy — From Survival to Manhood

I want to remember where I came from—not because I want to remain stuck in the past, but because I never want to forget what shaped me. I want to remember the boy I was, what he saw, what he lacked, what he endured, what he learned, and how he slowly became the man I am today.

## The boy who learned that life was not equal

My father passed away when I was young. After that, life became difficult for my family. Society created problems for my parents, and some members of the family who had more authority also created difficulties for us. I saw people with authority whose personal biases, fear, and lack of courage kept my family trapped in situations we did not deserve. Those circumstances affected our access to resources, our financial situation, and the opportunities available to us.

My parents were doing what they could. They worked hard, lived disciplined lives, and tried to give me whatever was possible. But they were financially weak. By the time I was around twelve, they had reached a point where they could no longer comfortably afford my education. My mother felt that she could not continue bearing my educational expenses after class eight, and I was sent to a hostel.

At twelve, I had to learn to live with limited resources.

While other students could buy snacks, food, treats, and other things, I often thought about saving money. I tried not to ask my parents for anything unless I genuinely needed it. I learned very early that money was not something I could take for granted; it determined what opportunities were available to me and what I had to give up.

I did not always understand why life had to be this way. I only knew that I had to adjust. I saw how friendships could change when people felt threatened or when their interests were involved, and I learned that there are situations in which you have to become your own support. I also chose not to spend much time pursuing sports as a possible career because I knew my parents could not afford to support that path.

At the same time, I saw my parents becoming stricter and, at times, emotionally distant. Their thinking became very practical: if I studied, got good marks, and built a career, I could carry myself forward because they could not do much more. I also saw how people around us influenced decisions that affected my education. For example, my parents were discouraged from buying me a smartphone that could have helped with my studies because people feared that it would affect my tenth-grade marks.

I slowly understood that my parents' limitations were not necessarily a lack of love. They were themselves trying to survive within the circumstances they had been given.

## When the boy needed someone to be there

Then came classes 11 and 12. During that period, I met someone who gave me emotional support. She was direct about her feelings and sometimes said things that made me feel that she was there for me. I took those words seriously because, at that point in my life, I genuinely needed that kind of support.

Later, I understood that when someone says, "I am there for you," it can simply be an expression of empathy and care at that moment. It does not necessarily mean that they will remain beside you through every difficulty or carry your problems with you.

She eventually left, while on the other side I was already struggling to complete my JEE preparation. My family was also dealing with serious problems around me, including situations involving domestic conflict and difficulties in accessing things we should reasonably have had. I was too young and emotionally inexperienced to process everything properly.

There were nights when I woke up with tears in my eyes because I felt that I had nothing and nobody with whom I could openly talk. I did not know how to explain the state of my mind to my parents or anyone else. Much of what I was going through remained inside me.

But I continued.

## College and the beginning of self-reliance

I joined college and understood that education was one of the few things I could use to change my circumstances. I worked as hard as I could to prepare myself for employment. I built projects, developed skills, and tried to create a future in which I would not have to depend on anyone's mercy.

Yet even after all that preparation, I was unable to get a job immediately.

That period taught me one of the most important lessons of my life: **everything is not within our control.** We can prepare ourselves, create favourable conditions, and increase the probability of a good outcome, but we cannot dictate exactly how or when that outcome will arrive.

I also started learning about subjects beyond my formal education because I was trying to understand myself and life better. I explored psychology, sociology, philosophy, theology, habits, personal finance, human behaviour, and other subjects. I was trying to understand how a person can rebuild himself when circumstances have already taken so much from him.

## Responsibility before stability

During the same period, my parents and maternal aunt became sick. I took them to Silchar and later to Hyderabad for medical treatment. For days, I had to stay with them because they could not manage everything inside the hospital by themselves. I also had my own health concerns, but I stayed because there were things that needed to be done.

Afterward, I learned another painful lesson: people can remember the one thing you did wrong while overlooking the many things you did right. I had helped with many things, yet sometimes the focus was placed on one mistake or one decision that could have been handled differently.

I had to learn not to let someone else's selective memory determine my understanding of my own character.

At the same time, the family problems had not disappeared, and the job situation remained uncertain. I again reached a point where I could not clearly see where my life was going. I needed an immediate source of income, so I worked wherever I could. I distributed pamphlets and worked at educational fairs as my brother's assistant.

I had reached a point where I could not simply wait for my education to rescue me. If education was not producing an opportunity immediately, I needed to find another way to earn.

For years, I had also depended on whatever other people were willing to share. During celebrations, someone might give me good food or clothes, and I was grateful because I knew my parents could not afford to provide those things. Sometimes, however, even that generosity came with conditions; people would offer food in exchange for work or give me food that was almost spoiled.

I accepted what was available because I did not have much choice.

Those experiences taught me gratitude, but they also taught me something I would understand more clearly later: **gratitude should not mean accepting exploitation forever.**

Eventually, my health began to suffer. I developed prehypertension and had a very poor lipid profile. Years of stress, limited resources, poor circumstances, and neglecting my own wellbeing had begun to show physically.

## Coming here and rebuilding myself

Then I came here.

Slowly, things began to change. I improved my health and fitness, became more disciplined, became more social, and learned to communicate better. I worked on my relationships with people and became more aware of my own behaviour, emotions, strengths, and weaknesses.

Most importantly, I changed as a person.

I became more open without wanting to be naive. I became more caring without wanting to become dependent. I became more social without needing everyone's approval. I learned that being close to someone does not give that person the right to dictate my life, and that helping someone does not mean I have to sacrifice myself completely.

I learned that I can trust people while still paying attention to their actions. I can forgive someone without giving them unlimited access to me. I can remain kind without allowing myself to be exploited, and I can stand up for myself without becoming cruel.

## The transition from boy to man

The boy initially learned that becoming a man meant being able to survive without anyone. Life eventually taught him something more mature.

Being a man is not simply about surviving alone. It is about learning to carry responsibility without losing your humanity. It is about protecting your family without controlling them, providing for them without believing that your entire worth comes from what you provide, and being strong enough to ask for help when you genuinely need it.

It is also about learning when to stay, when to leave, when to speak, when to remain silent, what belongs to you, and what you cannot control.

The boy once thought life was mainly about avoiding helplessness. The man understands that life must eventually become more than survival.

He wants to build security for his family because he knows what it feels like to grow up without it. He wants his future children to have opportunities that he did not have, but he does not want them to inherit only his survival instincts. He wants to give them security and freedom, discipline and affection, education and curiosity, responsibility and the ability to enjoy life.

He wants to teach them what hardship taught him without making them inherit the hardship itself.

## What I carry forward

I have seen scarcity, loss, family conflict, misuse of authority, changing friendships, loneliness, attachment, rejection, financial dependence, uncertainty, disappointment, and the frustration of working hard without immediately receiving the outcome I wanted.

But I have also learned from all of it.

Scarcity taught me discipline. Necessity taught me responsibility. Limited support taught me independence. Emotional pain taught me empathy. Difficult relationships taught me boundaries. Uncertainty taught me patience. Failure taught me humility. Repeated setbacks taught me that I can begin again.

The boy did not have all the knowledge I have today. He did not understand relationships, emotions, family problems, or uncertainty the way I understand them now. He made mistakes because he was still learning.

But he kept going.

That boy deserves compassion, not shame.

And the man he became has a responsibility toward him: to take care of his health, continue learning, build meaningful relationships, create financial security, and make use of the opportunities that the boy once wished he had.

Most importantly, he must not allow the hardship that shaped him to become the personality that controls him.

He came from scarcity, but he does not have to remain psychologically poor. He experienced uncertainty, but he does not have to control everything. He experienced rejection, but he does not have to beg to be chosen. He experienced people misusing authority, but he does not have to become authoritarian when he gains power. He experienced loneliness, but he does not have to close himself off from love.

He has survived enough.

Now he has to learn how to live.
